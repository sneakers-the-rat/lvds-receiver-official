## A photodiode receiver for the Miniscope Zero — no FPGA

The Miniscope Zero sends its video up an optical link: 8 Mbps,
Manchester-encoded, from LEDs on the head-mounted unit. The paper's
receiver for that link is a silicon-photomultiplier module wired to
a **desktop FPGA board**. This project replaces all of it with one
board built around a single avalanche photodiode:

- the light lands on the photodiode and comes out as **LVDS**, the
  same electrical form the original digitizer produced, and
- a built-in verification path recovers clock and data, decodes
  them in a microcontroller, and streams the result to **any laptop
  over USB-C** — plug it in, program it, and check the link
  yourself. Nothing else to buy, no FPGA toolchain.

## Status: design complete

The design was finished on 2026-11-10: schematic and layout
complete and fully routed (0 unconnected nets), the functional
simulations and firmware/host tests passing, the electrical rule
check at 0 errors, and fabrication files generated. A correction
pass on 2026-11-11 re-examined the design rule check from the
primary records: three real defects it had been masking were
found and fixed in the copper, the spacing rules were rewritten
on their proper standards basis, and the only items the checker
still reports are four board-edge items that are the USB-C
connector manufacturer's own land pattern — recorded as pinned
exclusions in the project file, named in the status record, and
counted by the CI gate rather than hidden.
What is **not** done is the part only hardware can do — nobody has
built one yet. The [bench test plan]({{ '/testplan/' | relative_url }})
lists, in order, everything the first build must prove: APD gain
and noise in the real circuit, shielding against the paper's
6.63 MHz wireless-power field, USB enumeration, and end-to-end bit
error rate against a real transmitter. The panel below is the
project's own build status, read from the same result files CI

## How it works

1. **Light in.** The C30737 avalanche photodiode sits beside the
   shield can (its amplifier neighbours live under the can, shielded
   from the arena's wireless-power field; the diode itself must see
   the light). An OPA657 transimpedance amplifier turns its current
   into voltage. In the standard build, a fitted 0 Ω link (R7)
   feeds that signal directly to an LMH7220 comparator, which
   slices it against a DAC-set threshold — the "adaptive
   digitizer" of the original, with the threshold under firmware
   control. The board also carries a THS4304 ×5 post-amplifier for
   bench experiments: use it by fitting R8 **instead of** R7,
   never both.
2. **LVDS out.** The comparator's differential output is the
   product: a bit-transparent LVDS pair on a header, ready to feed
   whatever the original FPGA board fed.
3. **Proof path.** The same signal is tapped into an ADN2813
   clock/data-recovery chip and an STM32H723, which captures the
   raw stream, decodes Manchester or 8b/10b in firmware, counts
   errors against the paper's PRBS-15 test pattern, and reports
   over USB. A DAC sets both the comparator threshold and the
   photodiode's bias voltage; a temperature sensor closes the
   bias-compensation loop.
4. **Safety bound in hardware.** Early in the design the bias
   supply could, on paper, demand far more than the detector's
   260 V breakdown limit. The shipped design cannot: component
   values bound the demand below the limit at any DAC setting,
   the converter stays off at power-up, and a physical jumper
   (J5) must be fitted before the high voltage can start at all.
   The evidence is written up in the repository (`docs/07`).

The full reasoning — requirements from the paper, part selection,
the power design, the risk register — lives in the repository's
`docs/` and is summarised on the [design]({{ '/design/' | relative_url }})
and [requirements]({{ '/requirements/' | relative_url }}) pages.

## How this site stays honest

Everything here is generated from the repository's own artefacts
by `scripts/site_data.py` at build time: the board renders and the
3D model are exported from the current board file, the BOM is
regenerated from the current schematic, and the status panel reads
the checkers' own result files — a section that cannot be produced
says so instead of showing a stale copy. The board's two design
checkers disagree by design and both are reported as-is: the
electrical rule check is a hard gate (0 errors), while the design
rule check counts adjacencies against the project's own wider
clearance classes — every one of them meets the 0.20 mm rule the
fab enforces, and the count is published rather than tuned away.

This project was designed and routed by an AI agent working for
the repository's owner, across 224 recorded layout passes; the
[routing history]({{ '/routing/' | relative_url }}) and
[token usage]({{ '/tokens/' | relative_url }}) pages are the
complete, unedited record of that process.
